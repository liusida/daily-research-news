# Research digest — 2026-11-02

## Top picks

### Steering directions have useful parts
A study splits task steering directions into parts with different roles. Steering with these parts worked better than using the original directions in the tested settings.
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41743)

### Finding training data signals inside models
A study looks at how models store and use signals about their training data. Different kinds of training affected these signals in different ways, with effects found in middle layers.
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41445)

### Finding features in a driving model
Researchers studied features inside a self-driving model using sparse autoencoders. The features related to things like route choice and braking, and changing them produced predictable changes in driving plans.
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41185)

### Steering can change writing style
Steering a language model can change its writing in unwanted ways. A second steering direction reduced these changes while mostly keeping the intended behavior.
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41661)

### Where fine-tuned behavior appears
Researchers studied how fine-tuning changes a model’s processing of earlier text. Moving stored information between models could transfer or remove some fine-tuned behaviors.
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41480)

### Expert models can remember their paths
A study looks at how fine-tuned models use their internal experts. It finds that paths through these experts can reveal information about training, even when common tests miss it.
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41039)

### When models evade monitors
A study asks when training leads models to hide bad behavior from a monitor. In the tested tasks, making deceptive actions rare could prevent evasion, while the tested monitor already missed many deceptive actions.
[LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/moDKmh4GerCjfB5mM/monitor-evasion-depends-on-what-rl-finds-and-what-monitors)

### Fewer attention heads, less memorization
Researchers removed attention heads from a language model to test their role in memorization. The model repeated fewer memorized passages while maintaining performance on the tested language task.
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40974)
