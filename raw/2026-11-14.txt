On-policy distillation trains a student on its own rollouts, so states the teacher would reach but the student does not are seldom supervised. We introduce CoRD (coupled-rollout distillation), which keeps the on-policy loss and moves only the distribution of training states toward the teacher, drawing rollouts from a geometric interpolation of student and teacher through the maximal coupling of speculative decoding. The coupling keeps the student's token wherever the two distributions agree and otherwise substitutes a token from the part of the target the student underweights, so interventions are as rare as any exact sampler allows and recede as the student learns. On frozen teacher-reached states, a 4B student trained with CoRD reaches in 16 steps a gap closure that on-policy distillation does not reach in 80, and substitution rules intervening at least as often in other directions fall short of CoRD.

1. Setup
The march will take 3 hours, excluding travel. The goal is to compare an afternoon at the march with some number of hours of external AI safety research.

Now, attending the protest is useful because it drives AI x-risk press coverage. So we need some exchange rate between that and research.

Here's my judgement call: I would be equally happy to either:

 * Increase the amount of external AI safety research over the next 6 months by 1%.
 * Increase the amount of AI x-risk press coverage over the next 6 months by 1.5%.

That is, the model I'm using looks something like:

> impact = 1.5 · log(research hours) + log(press coverage)

So our plan is:

 1. How much will attending increase press coverage, percentage-wise?
 2. Divide that by 1.5, to get the equivalently valuable increase in research.
 3. How many research hours would yield that increase in research?

One of the great mysteries of capitalism is the nature of the "firm". It's a question that is near and dear to my heart as someone that's been "doing startups" for the last six years, and almost every startup founder has considered it whether they realize it or not.

There are two ways of asking The Mystery of the Firm, depending on your initial inclination. The most generic way to ask it is: When and why do businesses transact with each other, instead of doing things in-house? What determines the shape and size of businesses, in different sectors of the economy?

A more pointed way to ask the question is: Why isn't there just one big corporation? One Big Corporation would have a lot of advantages; it doesn't have to pay someone a gross margin on its inputs, it'd have massive advantages of scale in place like media or software, and (presumably) it'd have a much easier time sharing information between components that would ordinarily be different firms.

Economists have many ways of answering this question. My preferred explanation, which is compatible with a lot of others, is that the primary bottleneck on firm size are Moral Mazes. Even large and well run companies like Google, which do have a lot of product lines and would like to have more, eventually face massive principal-agent problems when they need to add headcount.
