That includes the things you’d expect, like books and research papers. But it also includes microfilm archives, transcribed Betamax tapes, Donald Trump’s prenups, ancient scrolls and Harry Potter fanfiction. It’s a weird library.

The Epic of Gilgamesh
The Epic of Gilgamesh is considered the world's oldest written "great work of literature"; the earliest versions of it were written in 2100 BC in Sumer. The Sumerians did a fair amount of writing. Their medium was clay tablets. These tablets were designed to endure a few seasons of the Mesopotamian weather; this turned out to generalize well, and instead of surviving for a few years, they survived for millennia. Gilgamesh was lost in the sands of Iraq for a while, but was eventually dug up by European archeologists. The scholar who first translated the Epic to English got so excited by it that he took all of his clothes off and ran around the English museum shouting "I am the first man to read that after more than two thousand years of oblivion."

I started writing this essay to share some thoughts on how to reconcile the idea of circuits with gradient descent. In doing this, I've come to change my perspective pretty substantially. The first half of this essay engages a Darwinian picture of circuits, but I think the central point - that we need a clearer ontology of computation in neural networks - holds without reference thereto.

Before, I was thinking about circuits as 'units of selection' - the kinds of things which engage in some gradient-hacking shenanigans to save themselves from gradient descent. I don't think this picture is unimportant or unhelpful, but I've come to think that it sidesteps the hard part of the problem - that the things the circuit Darwinism picture takes for granted are the confused parts, and I think therefore the more insightful to examine. I have gone from thinking "circuit Darwinism, but we need to formalize how to delineate the circuits" to "things that under circuit Darwinism seem like implementation details are actually the deep source of confusion, and we should treat these as the problem".
